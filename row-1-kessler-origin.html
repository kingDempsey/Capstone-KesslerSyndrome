NASA Johnson Space Center (1978) • JGR Vol. 83, No. A6

Collision Frequency of Artificial Satellites:
The Creation of a Debris Belt

Authors: Donald J. Kessler & Burton G. Cour-Palais
Primary Altitude Focus: 700 km – 1200 km LEO
Baseline Catalog (1976): 3,866 Tracked Satellites

Paper Core Thesis

"As the number of artificial satellites in earth orbit increases, the probability of collisions between satellites also increases. Satellite collisions produce orbiting fragments, each of which increases the probability of further collisions, leading to the growth of a belt of debris around the earth."

In 1978, Donald J. Kessler and Burton G. Cour-Palais applied mathematical models originally developed to study asteroid belt evolution to low Earth orbit (LEO). They proved that hypervelocity satellite collisions would eventually become the primary source of new space debris—overtaking natural meteoroids and direct launch additions—triggering an un-stoppable cascade if launch practices remained unconstrained[cite: 2, 6, 7].

Phase 1

1. Spatial Density & Relative Velocity Models

To model collision frequency, Kessler & Cour-Palais first established spatial density \(S\) (satellites per unit volume) as a function of altitude \(R\) and geocentric latitude \(\beta\)[cite: 2]. By dividing low Earth space into volume elements (\(\Delta R = 50\text{ km}\), \(\Delta \beta = 3^\circ\)), spatial density is calculated by summing the probability of finding each satellite within that slice[cite: 2]:

$$dI/dt = S \cdot \bar{V}_s \cdot A_c$$

Equation (1): Single Spacecraft Impact Rate. Where \(dI/dt\) is the impact rate, \(S\) is local spatial density, \(\bar{V}_s\) is average relative velocity (\(\sim 7\text{ km/s}\)), and \(A_c\) is spacecraft cross-sectional area.

$$\frac{dC}{dt} = \frac{1}{2} \int S^2 \bar{V}_s \bar{A}_{cc} \, dU$$

Equation (2): Total System Collision Rate. Where \(C\) is total mutual collisions, \(\bar{A}_{cc}\) is average collision cross-section (\(\sim 4\text{ m}^2\)), and \(dU\) is the volume element. Notice the square dependence on spatial density (\(S^2\)), driving exponential feedback.

Phase 2

2. Hypervelocity Fragmentation & Energy Ratios

At an average impact velocity of 10 km/s (\(\bar{V}_c\)), collisions occur in the hypervelocity regime[cite: 3, 4]. Instantaneous impact pressures exceed \(10^{12} \text{ dyn/cm}^2\), generating intense shockwaves, localized vaporization, cratering, and catastrophic fragmentation[cite: 4, 5].

Class I

Non-Catastrophic Impact

Occurs when Target Mass \(M_2\) is significantly larger than Projectile Mass \(M_1\) (\(M_2 > \Gamma' M_1\)). The target survives, but ejects crater mass \(M_e\)[cite: 5]:

$$M_e = \Gamma M_1 \quad (\Gamma \approx 115 \text{ for spacecraft})$$
Class II

Catastrophic Disruption

Occurs when Projectile Mass is sufficient to completely shatter the structural target (\(M_2 < \Gamma' M_1\))[cite: 5]. Ejected mass equals total system mass[cite: 5]:

$$M_e = M_1 + M_2$$

Fragment Power-Law Distribution

Extrapolating hypervelocity lab tests into spacecraft structures (Langley Research Center), the cumulative number of fragments \(N\) of mass \(M\) or larger produced in a single collision obeys[cite: 5]:

$$N = 0.8 \left( \frac{M}{M_e} \right)^{-0.8}$$

Across the satellite population, Kessler calculated an average mass yield of 870 kg of ejected fragments per collision, with velocities ranging from 10 to 30 m/s relative to the parent body[cite: 5, 6].

Historical & Scientific Arc

Chronology of the Debris Cascade Theory

1957

Sputnik 1 Launch & Early LEO Accumulation

The launch of artificial satellites begins. Initial space operations treat Low Earth Orbit as an infinite sink, leaving spent rocket upper stages and non-operational payloads in orbit with no disposal protocols.

1971

Asteroid Belt Collision Models Derived

Donald J. Kessler publishes mathematical models estimating particle densities and collision risks in the natural asteroid belt (NASA SP-267)[cite: 2, 3, 11]. Techniques for evaluating spatial density integrals over inclined orbits are established.

1976

PAR Radar Test & Catalog Under-counting Uncovered

A 12-hour point-in-time sampling test by the Perimeter Acquisition Radar (PAR) reveals that the Space Defense Center (SDC) catalog of 3,866 objects is deficient by at least 18%, failing to track sub-10cm objects and unrecorded rocket body explosions[cite: 2, 3].

JUNE 1978

Publication of Landmark JGR Paper

Kessler and Cour-Palais publish "Collision Frequency of Artificial Satellites: The Creation of a Debris Belt" in the Journal of Geophysical Research[cite: 2]. They formally predict that self-sustaining collisional feedback will exceed natural meteoroids by the late 1990s[cite: 2, 4, 6].

1996

First Confirmed Hypervelocity Collision in Orbit

The French reconnaissance satellite Cerise is struck and partially severed by a cataloged fragment from an exploded Ariane rocket stage at 14 km/s relative speed, validating Kessler's mathematical predictions of fragment risks.

2009

Iridium 33 - Cosmos 2251 Hypervelocity Collision

An active operational communications satellite (Iridium 33) collides with a defunct military satellite (Cosmos 2251) at 789 km altitude at 11.7 km/s, producing over 2,000 trackable large fragments and thousands of untrackable micro-debris particles.
